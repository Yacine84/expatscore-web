Savings, ETFs, and pensions: Essential money guide for young expats in Germany

How saving and investing work in Germany: Tagesgeld and Festgeld for your cash buffer, low-cost ETF savings plans and their taxes — including the €1,000 allowance and the Vorabpauschale — plus the statutory, company and private pension layers, in a simple order you can follow.

📅 May 11, 2026 ⏱ 9 min read ✍ ExpatScore Editorial
← Back to Blog

As a young expat in Germany, navigating the country's financial system can be daunting. With so many options available, it's essential to understand the basics of savings, investments, and pensions to secure your financial future. In this article, we'll delve into the world of savings, ETFs, and pensions, providing you with a clear understanding of how to manage your finances in Germany.

Understanding Savings Options in Germany

Every money plan in Germany starts with a cash layer: money that is safe, reachable and boring. Two account types do almost all of that work, alongside the traditional savings book:

  • Tagesgeld: An instant-access savings account. Your money is available any day; the interest rate is variable and can move up or down.
  • Festgeld: A fixed-term deposit. You lock money away for an agreed term (often 6 months to a few years) in exchange for a fixed, usually slightly higher rate; early withdrawal is normally not possible or costs you the interest.
  • Sparbuch: The traditional savings book. Familiar and safe, but rates are usually the lowest of the three and withdrawals above a monthly limit need notice.

Whichever you choose, deposits at banks in the EU are protected by deposit insurance (Einlagensicherung) up to €100,000 per customer, per bank — if the bank fails, you are repaid up to that limit. That safety is why Tagesgeld and Festgeld are the natural home for your emergency fund: money you might need at short notice does not belong in the stock market. A common rule of thumb is three to six months of essential expenses, built up before you invest anything.

Investing in ETFs as an Expat in Germany

Once the buffer exists, investing is how your money outgrows inflation. The standard vehicle for expats is an ETF savings plan (Sparplan): many German brokers execute a monthly plan with no order fee, with minimums from €1 to €25 a month, so starting matters far more than the amount.

Most simple portfolios are built around one broadly diversified world equity ETF as the core. The ongoing fund costs (TER) for core world ETFs are typically around 0.1–0.3% per year. When you compare ETFs, look at:

  • Diversification: hundreds or thousands of companies, ideally spread worldwide, rather than one country or one fashionable sector.
  • TER: the lower the ongoing cost, the less drag on your returns every single year.
  • Fund size and age: larger, established funds are less likely to be closed or merged away.
  • Tracking quality: how closely the fund follows its index in practice.

A single world ETF that ticks these boxes is a strategy; a basket of niche theme funds usually is not. And only invest money you will not need for at least ten years — equity ETFs can fall sharply in the short term, which is what your Tagesgeld buffer protects you from.

ETF Taxes in Germany: What Is Deducted, and When

Germany taxes capital income — dividends, realised gains and the advance tax described below — at a flat rate: 25% withholding tax (Abgeltungsteuer) plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge, which makes 26.375%, plus church tax if you are a church member. If you are tax-resident in Germany and use a German broker, the broker calculates and deducts the tax automatically. You do not have to report each trade yourself.

The first €1,000 of capital income per year (€2,000 for married couples filing jointly) is tax-free — the Sparer-Pauschbetrag. But it is not applied automatically: you must give your broker a Freistellungsauftrag (exemption order) up to that amount. Without one, the broker withholds tax from the first euro, and you can only reclaim it through a tax return. Setting the order takes a few minutes in your broker's app and is the most valuable five minutes in this whole guide.

Equity funds and ETFs (at least 51% invested in shares) get a further break: the Teilfreistellung exempts 30% of the gain, so you are taxed on only 70% of it.

Accumulating ETFs pay out no dividends, so Germany taxes a deemed return instead — the Vorabpauschale. It is calculated as the fund's value on 1 January × the official base interest rate (Basiszins: 2.53% for 2025, 3.20% for 2026) × 0.7, and it is capped at the fund's actual gain that year. A worked example: a €10,000 accumulating equity ETF gives a base amount of €224 (€10,000 × 3.20% × 0.7); after the 30% Teilfreistellung, €156.80 is taxable — roughly €41 of tax if your €1,000 allowance is already used up. If your allowance is untouched, you pay €0: at the 2026 base rate, an equity-ETF portfolio only starts producing a Vorabpauschale bill above roughly €63,800 for a single investor. The tax is debited each January for the previous year, and amounts already taxed this way are credited when you eventually sell, so the same gain is never taxed twice. Practical tip: keep a small cash buffer in your settlement account every January so the debit does not fail.

Understanding Pensions as an Expat in Germany

The first pension layer is automatic. As an employee you pay 18.6% of your gross salary into the statutory pension (gesetzliche Rentenversicherung) — half deducted from your pay, half added by your employer. You cannot opt out, and the contributions are not lost on expats: they build pension entitlements, and 60 months of contributions is also one of the conditions for a permanent residence permit. But the statutory pension replaces only part of your working income — it is a base, not a full plan.

The second layer is a company pension (betriebliche Altersvorsorge). If you convert part of your salary into one (Entgeltumwandlung), your employer must add at least 15% on top of the amount you convert — a legal minimum subsidy that is easy to leave on the table if you never ask HR. Whether the specific contract your employer offers is good value still depends on its costs, so look before you sign.

The third layer is private, state-subsidised provision. The Riester pension is being replaced for new contracts from 1 January 2027 by a new product, the Altersvorsorgedepot; existing Riester contracts are grandfathered and can simply continue. Subsidised and insurance-based pension products come with contract terms and extra cost layers that a plain ETF plan does not — our guide to retirement savings cost traps shows what to check before signing.

That difference matters especially for expats: a self-managed ETF depot has no contract lock-in, minimal costs, and can simply be sold or taken with you if you leave Germany. German subsidised pension contracts are the products with strings attached if your life moves abroad.

A Simple Starter Order

If the system feels like a lot, this is the order that works for most expats:

  1. Build the buffer: three to six months of essential expenses in a Tagesgeld account, protected up to €100,000 per bank.
  2. Clear expensive debt: consumer credit and overdrafts cost more interest than investments reliably earn.
  3. Start the ETF plan: one broad world ETF, monthly, low TER — and set your Freistellungsauftrag the same day.
  4. Check the subsidised options: ask HR about the company pension and the 15% employer top-up before signing any pension contract.

That sequence is deliberately boring. In personal finance, boring and started beats clever and postponed.

This article is general information about how the German system works, not financial advice. For decisions about your own money, a fee-only adviser or a consumer advice centre (Verbraucherzentrale) can look at your full picture.
