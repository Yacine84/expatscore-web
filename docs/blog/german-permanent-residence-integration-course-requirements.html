Mastering Germany's Integration Course for Permanent Residence

Here are the full requirements, the fast tracks, and how to apply.

The Niederlassungserlaubnis: what permanent residence actually gives you

The Niederlassungserlaubnis (settlement permit) is Germany's unlimited residence title. Once you have it, your right to live in Germany no longer depends on a job, a degree or a temporary purpose of stay: there are no more permit renewals, you can work for any employer or become self-employed, and your status survives changes in your personal situation.

It is permanent — but not unconditional. The permit generally lapses if you leave Germany for more than six months in a row without getting permission from the Ausländerbehörde in advance. If a longer stay abroad is coming up — a work assignment, caring for family — ask for that permission before you go, not after.

Worth knowing: there is a sister title, the Erlaubnis zum Daueraufenthalt-EU (EU long-term residence permit). Its conditions are very similar, but it additionally lets you move to and work in most other EU countries under simplified conditions. It is not granted automatically — you have to ask for it specifically, so mention it when you apply if EU mobility matters to you.

The standard route: five years and the full requirements checklist

Most expats qualify under §9 of the Residence Act (Aufenthaltsgesetz). The clock starts with your first residence permit — effectively with your Anmeldung after arrival — and after five years you must meet every condition on this list:

  • You have held a residence permit for five years.
  • Your livelihood is secured: you can support yourself (and your family) from your own income, without relying on Bürgergeld or similar public benefits.
  • You have paid 60 months of contributions to the statutory pension insurance (gesetzliche Rentenversicherung). Periods spent raising children or caring for relatives can count towards the 60 months — and in a marriage, your spouse's contributions can be enough.
  • Your German is at level B1.
  • You have basic knowledge of the legal and social order and of living conditions in Germany — normally proven with the "Leben in Deutschland" test.
  • You have sufficient living space for yourself and your family — as a rule of thumb, about 12 m² per person aged six and over, and about 10 m² per younger child.
  • You are permitted to work — your current permit allows employment.
  • You have no serious criminal record; serious offences can block the permit.

Two items on that list are pure "knowledge" proofs: B1 German and the legal-order test. The next two sections explain how to get them — because the integration course delivers both in a single certificate.

How the integration course covers two requirements at once

The BAMF integration course (Integrationskurs) is built exactly around these two proofs. It combines a language course of 600 teaching hours — aimed at level B1 and ending with the DTZ exam (Deutsch-Test für Zuwanderer) — with a 100-hour orientation course (Orientierungskurs) on German law, history and values, which ends with the "Leben in Deutschland" test. That makes 700 teaching hours in total.

Pass the DTZ at B1 level and the Leben in Deutschland test, and you receive the Zertifikat Integrationskurs. For the settlement permit, this one certificate is accepted as proof of both the B1 language requirement and the knowledge-of-the-legal-order requirement — no separate language exam and no separate civics test. That is why the course is the standard route for applicants who do not yet have B1.

What does it cost? Participants normally pay a contribution of €2.29 per teaching hour — about €1,603 for the complete course — usually charged in instalments per module. If you receive benefits such as Bürgergeld, or in hardship cases, you can be exempted from the contribution on application; retaking a module costs extra. Who is entitled to a subsidised place (Berechtigung) depends on your residence status and when you arrived in Germany; other newcomers can often be admitted if free places remain. The BAMF website lists course providers and current courses near you.

Already speak German? The two proofs without the course

The integration course is not compulsory for the settlement permit. If your German is already at B1 or above, any recognised B1 certificate — for example from a Goethe-Institut or telc exam — satisfies the language requirement on its own.

For the knowledge requirement, you simply book the "Leben in Deutschland" test on its own at a test centre. It consists of 33 multiple-choice questions, and you need at least 17 correct answers to pass. The questions come from a fixed pool — 300 general questions plus 10 about your Bundesland — the same pool used for the citizenship test, and BAMF provides free official practice material and a model test online. Bring the two certificates to your appointment and you are exactly as well prepared as a course graduate.

The fast tracks: permanent residence in two or three years

Depending on your category, you may not have to wait the full five years. The main shortcuts:

  • Skilled workers (§18c): after 3 years, with 36 months of pension contributions and B1 German.
  • People who completed a degree or vocational training in Germany: after 2 years, with 24 months of pension contributions.
  • EU Blue Card holders: after 27 months with A1 German — or just 21 months if you have B1.
  • Self-employed people (§21): after 3 years.
  • Spouses of German citizens (§28): after 3 years.
  • Recognised refugees (§26): 5 years as standard — or 3 years with exceptional integration, meaning C1 German and being largely self-sufficient.

The fast tracks shorten the clock; they do not remove the other conditions. Your livelihood must still be secured, and the language and knowledge proofs are still required at the level stated.

How to apply: documents, fees and timing

You apply at the Ausländerbehörde (foreigners' authority) of the city where you live. Book the appointment early and apply before your current permit expires: processing takes from a few weeks to several months. If your permit runs out while your application is being processed, the authority issues a Fiktionsbescheinigung — a temporary certificate that keeps your stay legal while you wait.

Bring these documents:

  • Valid passport and your current residence permit
  • Proof of income: employment contract, your last three payslips and a recent confirmation from your employer
  • Your pension insurance record (Rentenversicherungsverlauf) from the Deutsche Rentenversicherung, showing your contribution months
  • B1 language proof and the Leben in Deutschland result — or the Zertifikat Integrationskurs covering both
  • Proof of living space: your tenancy agreement and, if asked, a confirmation of the flat's size from your landlord
  • A biometric passport photo

The application fee is roughly €113 to €150 depending on your category. If anything is missing, you will normally receive a written list of what to submit — respond quickly so that your file is not closed.

Quick tip: The complete official question catalogue for the Leben in Deutschland test — all 300 general questions plus your state's 10 — is available free on the BAMF website, including a model test. It is the same pool the orientation course prepares you for, so you can start practising long before your course or test date.
