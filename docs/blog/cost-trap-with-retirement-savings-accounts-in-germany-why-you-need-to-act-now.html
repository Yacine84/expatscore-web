Cost trap with retirement savings accounts in Germany: Why you need to act now

German pension contracts load most of their costs into the first five years — so your balance can be worth less than you paid in. Here is how to check your own contract in about 15 minutes, and how to escape a bad one without handing your state allowances back.

📅 May 11, 2026 ⏱ 10 min read ✍ ExpatScore Editorial
← Back to Blog

As an expat living in Germany, navigating the landscape of retirement savings accounts can be daunting. The main products — Riester-Rente, Rürup-Rente (Basisrente), company pensions (betriebliche Altersvorsorge, or bAV) and plain private pension insurance — are often sold with glossy projections and very little talk about costs. Yet the costs are exactly where these contracts differ, and they compound silently for decades. This guide names the traps with the real mechanisms behind them, shows you how to check your own contract in about 15 minutes, and lays out the exits that fix the problem without throwing away subsidies you have already earned.

The Cost Traps, Concretely: Where Your Money Actually Leaks

Trap 1: the costs are front-loaded into the first five years. For classic and fund-based insurance and Riester contracts, the acquisition and distribution costs — the commission for selling you the contract — are spread over the first five years of the term. The consequence is brutal and simple: in the early years, your surrender value (Rückkaufswert, what you would actually get back if you exited) is typically well below the sum of the contributions you have paid in. The trap stays invisible because your annual statement shows what you paid in, not this gap.

Trap 2: the total cost hides in one small figure. Every contract has a Produktinformationsblatt (product information sheet) that states the Effektivkosten — the annual reduction in your yield caused by all costs together, fund costs and contract costs combined. Consumer advisers flag products whose effective costs sit clearly above what comparable products charge, because a difference of one or two percentage points per year decides whether the contract beats a simple savings plan at all. As a comparison anchor: a plain ETF savings plan typically has total running costs of around 0.1–0.3% per year for core world ETFs.

Trap 3: the "guaranteed" pension factor that isn't. Many contracts quote a Rentenfaktor — the factor that later converts your capital into a monthly pension. In the fine print, insurers often reserve the right to adjust this factor. A pension that looks guaranteed on the sales brochure can shrink before you ever receive it.

Trap 4: the guarantee that eats the return. A large guarantee component (for example, a guarantee that you at least get your contributions back) sounds safe, but it forces the provider to invest very conservatively. You buy that safety with decades of low returns — which, after costs, can leave the contract barely ahead of a current account.

How to Check Your Own Contract in 15 Minutes

You do not need an adviser to find out whether your contract is a trap. You need four documents and a quarter of an hour:

  1. Find the Produktinformationsblatt. It came with your contract documents; if you have lost it, your provider must supply it. Read the Effektivkosten figure.
  2. Benchmark that figure. Compare it with the 0.1–0.3% per year of a plain ETF savings plan, and with cheaper certified Riester contracts if yours is a Riester product. Clearly higher effective costs are the first red flag.
  3. Ask for your current surrender value (Rückkaufswert) — in writing. Compare it with the total contributions you have paid in so far. The gap between the two is the real price of the contract's cost structure to date.
  4. Request an allowance statement. Ask the allowance office (Zulagenstelle) or the German Pension Insurance for an Auskunft showing exactly how much state money (Zulagen) and tax advantage sits in your contract. That amount is what a cancellation would claw back — you need the real number before any decision.
  5. Check the Rentenfaktor clause. Is your pension factor truly guaranteed, or can the insurer adjust it? One sentence in the conditions answers this.

If the picture is still unclear, the consumer advice centres (Verbraucherzentralen) will review a pension contract independently for a modest fee. This article is general information, not financial advice — but an independent review is a far better basis for a decision than the projections in a sales folder.

Why Cancelling Is Usually the Worst Exit

Once people discover the cost trap, the instinct is to cancel and start over. With a subsidised Riester contract, that is normally the most expensive move available. Early cancellation counts as schädliche Verwendung (harmful use, §93 EStG), and the state takes its money back in full: all allowances (Zulagen) you ever received, plus all the tax advantages from the special-expenses deduction, are deducted from your balance. What reaches your account is only the surrender value minus those repayments, minus tax on the investment earnings. After a long-running contract, the repayments can eat most or all of the balance — the pension insurance's own guidance warns that some savers end up with nothing, or even owing money. Even the payout takes time: expect weeks, up to roughly two months, while the allowance office settles the clawback.

In other words: the contract may be mediocre, but cancelling it converts a mediocre outcome into a guaranteed loss. Use one of the exits below instead.

The Escape Routes That Keep Your Money

In rough order of preference:

  1. Transfer to a cheaper contract (Anbieterwechsel). Move the balance into another certified Riester contract — for example a low-cost fund-based one. The transfer fee is €50–150, and crucially, your allowances stay fully intact because the money never leaves the subsidised system.
  2. Make the contract contribution-free (beitragsfrei stellen). Stop paying in. The balance stays invested, and — unlike cancellation — allowances already received are not clawed back; you simply receive no new allowances while paused. This is always possible, works in a cash crunch, and is reversible if your situation improves.
  3. Reduce your contributions. If the problem is the monthly budget rather than the contract itself, lowering the contribution keeps the contract (and some funding) alive.
  4. Switch the underlying funds. In fund-based contracts, changing the fund selection is often free and can change your long-term returns materially — without touching the contract or its subsidies.
  5. Claim allowances you have missed. Before any structural decision, check for money already left on the table: allowances are frequently unclaimed because the standing allowance application (Dauerzulagenantrag) was never filed, or because life changes — children, income — were never reported to the provider.

Special Cases: Contracts You Cannot Simply Cancel

Rürup / Basisrente: a basic pension contract cannot be cancelled, assigned or borrowed against at all — that is fixed by law. Pausing or reducing contributions is the only lever you have, which is why the cost check matters twice as much before signing one.

Company pension (Direktversicherung / bAV): these generally cannot be cancelled either. When you change jobs, the normal options are to take the contract to the new employer, transfer its value, or leave it paused. And a fairness point: not every old contract is a trap. An older Direktversicherung with a high guaranteed interest rate (2% or more) and low administrative costs can be genuinely worth keeping — the numbers from your 15-minute check, not the contract's age, should decide.

The expat portability point: subsidised German contracts are designed for retiring in Germany. Leaving the EU/EEA permanently can also count as harmful use for a Riester contract if the money is then paid out or used abroad. If there is a realistic chance your retirement happens outside Europe, weigh that before signing a contract designed to run for 30 years — and read the fine print on what happens when you leave.

The 2027 Reform: Review Now, Don't Panic

So why "act now"? Because the system changes soon — but not in a way that should scare you into cancelling. From 1 January 2027, the Riester system is replaced for new contracts by the new Altersvorsorgedepot. Existing contracts have grandfathering (Bestandsschutz): yours can simply continue as it is. And from 2027, holders of existing contracts can also switch their contract's funding into the new system, or transfer the balance into a new-style contract, without repaying past allowances.

The actionable conclusion is the opposite of panic: review your contract now, while every transfer route preserves your allowances. Cancelling in a rush before 2027 is precisely the one move that destroys them.
