Two paths: learn from scratch or convert your licence

If you already hold a driving licence from your home country, the most expensive mistake you can make in Germany is signing up for a full driving course before checking whether you need one at all. Expats have two completely different routes to a German licence (Führerschein), and they differ by thousands of euros:

  • Path A — start from scratch: the full German training with theory course, practice lessons and both exams. Realistic total: roughly €2,500-4,500.
  • Path B — convert your foreign licence (Umschreibung): an administrative process at the licence office. Depending on your country you face no exams at all, one exam, or both — but never the mandatory training. Typical total: €100-500.

Which path applies to you depends on where your licence was issued, not on how well you drive. Check conversion first; the breakdown below shows what each route really costs.

Path A: the full cost of learning from scratch

German driver training is thorough and heavily regulated, which is why it is expensive. For a class B (car) licence you complete a theory course, a set of mandatory special lessons, and as many practice lessons as your instructor thinks you need. You also pay for every exam twice over: the official TÜV or DEKRA fee, plus the driving school's own presentation fee (Vorstellungsentgelt) for putting you forward.

Where the money goes. The single biggest variable is the number of practice lessons: at €55-95 each, the difference between 15 and 25 lessons is up to €950. The second risk is failing an exam — every failed attempt repeats the exam fee and the presentation fee, and most schools will insist on extra lessons before the next try. Schools in large cities also charge noticeably more per lesson than schools in smaller towns, so the same licence can cost over €1,000 more in Munich than in a rural district.

One relief for expats: the theory exam is offered in English, Arabic and several other languages, so your German does not need to be exam-ready for the written test. The practical exam is conducted in German, although the examiner's commands are simple directions rather than conversation.

Path B: converting your foreign licence (Umschreibung)

The six-month rule. A licence from an EU or EEA country is recognised in Germany for as long as it is valid — no conversion, no exams; you only exchange it if you want a German card, for example when it expires. A licence from anywhere else lets you drive for six months from the day you take up residence in Germany — in practice, from your Anmeldung (address registration). After those six months the licence stops being recognised, and driving on it counts as driving without a licence: a criminal offence, not a fine. The licence office can extend the deadline by up to six months, but only if you can prove that your total stay in Germany will not exceed twelve months.

Countries on the exemption list (Anlage 11). The Fahrerlaubnis-Verordnung's Anlage 11 — the Staatenliste — lists the non-EU countries whose licences Germany treats more generously. Depending on the country (and, for the USA, the individual state), you convert with no exams at all, only the theory exam, or only the practical exam. Many US states, Canada, Australia, Japan, South Korea and Switzerland are on the list, and it changes over time — Ukraine and Montenegro were added in August 2026. Always check the current list for your exact country before you book anything.

Countries not on the list. You must pass both the theory and the practical exam — but that is all. There are no mandatory lessons and no minimum hours. You register with a driving school, because only a school can present you for the practical exam, prepare for the theory test with an app, and most experienced drivers take a few practice lessons to learn what German examiners look for.

Documents and cost. For the conversion you need your passport, your registration certificate (Meldebescheinigung), the foreign licence with a certified translation (an International Driving Permit can serve as the translation), an eye test, a first-aid certificate and a biometric photo. The licence office fee is roughly €37.50-45.90; add the translation and, where required, the exam fees, and the whole conversion typically comes to €100-500 — around a tenth of the from-scratch price. One detail to know: depending on how long you have already held your original licence, the converted licence may or may not come with the two-year probationary period (Probezeit).

Hidden costs — and how to keep the bill down

  • Compare lesson prices, not base fees. A school advertising a €350 base fee but charging €90 per lesson will cost you more than a €550 school charging €60. Ask for the complete price list — schools are required to display their prices.
  • Ask how many lessons they really expect. Before signing, ask for a realistic estimate for someone with your experience, and what their average learner pays in total.
  • Never sit an exam you are not ready for. A failed practical repeats the ~€130 exam fee plus up to €289 in presentation fees, plus extra lessons. One more week of practice is cheaper than one failed attempt.
  • Start the conversion early. Begin the Umschreibung in your second or third month, not your sixth. If you need exams and the deadline passes while you wait for a retake, you cannot legally drive in the meantime.
  • Take the theory app seriously. The official question catalogue is large but finite. Passing first time saves a repeated €25 exam fee and €60-137 in presentation fees.

And the simplest saving of all: if your licence can be converted, convert it. Even the most expensive conversion — both exams, translation and a few practice lessons — rarely exceeds €500, while the full course starts at around €2,500.

Quick tip: Before you sign anything at a driving school, check Anlage 11 of the Fahrerlaubnis-Verordnung for your country — or ask the Führerscheinstelle — and confirm whether you can convert your licence. Ten minutes of checking can save you €2,000 or more.
