Apply for a German Working Holiday Visa: A Step-by-Step Guide

Twelve months of living and travelling in Germany, funded by temporary jobs — if you hold the right passport. This guide covers the 12 eligible countries, the age and money rules, and the exact application process step by step.

What the Working Holiday Visa is

The Working Holiday Visa lets young travellers from Germany's partner countries live in Germany for up to 12 months and take temporary jobs to help fund the trip. It is based on bilateral Working Holiday agreements — with Canada, the arrangement is called the Youth Mobility Programme — and it is designed for cultural exchange, not for starting a career or settling in Germany.

That purpose shapes how the visa works: the holiday is the main reason for your stay, and the work is meant to top up your travel budget along the way. You can work for any employer, full-time or part-time, but the stay is a one-off. The visa runs for a maximum of 12 months, it cannot be extended, and each person can take part only once. You also cannot bring dependent family members or children with you on this visa.

Who is eligible: the 12 countries and the age limit

Only citizens of the 12 countries that have a Working Holiday agreement with Germany can apply:

  • Argentina
  • Australia
  • Brazil
  • Chile
  • Hong Kong SAR
  • Israel
  • Japan
  • Canada
  • New Zealand
  • South Korea
  • Taiwan
  • Uruguay

If your country is not on this list, there is no Working Holiday route for you — you would need a regular work, study or job-seeker permit instead.

Age: you must be between 18 and 30 years old, and the visa has to be granted before your 31st birthday. The exception: citizens of Australia and Canada can apply up to the age of 35. You also need a valid passport from your home country, and you must be a first-time participant — anyone who has already held a German Working Holiday Visa cannot get a second one. Some of the agreements include annual quotas, so if your country has one, applying early in the year improves your chances. The German embassy or consulate in your home country publishes the exact checklist for your nationality; always check it before you book your appointment.

The money and insurance requirements

Proof of funds. You must show at least €2,000 in savings, proven with recent bank statements in your name. Depending on the embassy or consulate, expect the real expectation to be closer to €3,000 — the more you can show above the minimum, the smoother the application. Credit-card statements are not accepted as proof of funds.

You also need a return ticket — or, if you do not have one yet, additional money on top of the savings above to prove that you can buy one.

Unlike the student visa, no blocked account (Sperrkonto) is required for this visa: ordinary bank statements are the standard proof of funds.

Insurance. Comprehensive travel and health insurance covering your entire stay in Germany is mandatory. The policy must be comparable to statutory German health insurance and must cover medical treatment, hospitalisation and medical repatriation to your home country. Very basic backpacker policies with low coverage caps are a common reason for problems at the appointment — bring the full policy documents, not just a booking confirmation.

The application process, step by step

For most nationalities, the application is made in person at the German embassy or consulate in your home country, and you can apply at most three months before your planned entry date.

  1. Check your embassy's checklist. Requirements differ slightly by country, and some agreements have annual quotas. The mission's website is the authoritative source for your nationality.
  2. Gather your documents. Typically: the completed application form, a valid passport, biometric passport photos, proof of funds (bank statements), your insurance policy documents, and your return ticket or proof of the extra funds to buy one. Documents that are not in German or English need a German translation.
  3. Book and attend your appointment. Applications are lodged in person. Book early — in high season, appointment slots at popular missions fill up weeks ahead.
  4. Wait for the decision. Once approved, your passport comes back with the Working Holiday Visa in it. If you applied in advance, the visa covers the full 12 months and you can start working from your first day in Germany.

The exception — apply after you arrive: citizens of Australia, Canada, Israel, Japan and New Zealand may enter Germany without a visa and apply for the residence permit at the local immigration office (Ausländerbehörde) instead. There is a catch: you are not allowed to start any job until the permit has actually been issued, and Ausländerbehörde appointments can involve long waits. The embassies therefore recommend applying in advance whenever possible.

Quick tip: Two dates control everything — you can apply at most 3 months before you enter Germany, and the visa must be granted before your 31st birthday (35th for Australians and Canadians). If your birthday is close, apply as early as the window allows.

Working rules: what jobs you can do, and for how long

With the Working Holiday Visa you may work for any employer in Germany, full-time or part-time — in a café, a warehouse, a hotel, on a farm. What you may not do is work self-employed: freelancing, contracting and running your own business are not permitted under the programme.

Some agreements cap how long you may work. The limits that catch people out:

  • Argentina: you may work for a total of 6 months during your stay.
  • Chile and Uruguay: a maximum of 6 months with the same employer.
  • Israel: a maximum of 3 months with the same employer.
  • New Zealand: you may work up to 12 months for the same employer.

Every job must pay at least the German minimum wage — €13.90 per hour in 2026. Typical working-holiday jobs are in hospitality, warehouses and logistics, seasonal farm work and tourism. Note that au pair placements are a separate scheme with their own visa rules — do not confuse the two.

Your first two weeks in Germany

However you applied, your first admin task after arriving is the same as for everyone who moves to Germany: register your address at the local citizens' office (Bürgeramt) within 14 days — the Anmeldung. The registration certificate (Meldebescheinigung) is what you need to open a German bank account, which most employers require in order to pay your salary.

Shortly after registering, your personal tax ID (Steuer-ID) is sent to you by post. Every employer will ask for this number — our Steuer-ID guide explains what it is and what to do if it does not arrive. Keep your insurance documents handy, too: if an employer or an office asks how you are covered, your policy is the proof.

Then the fun part: job hunting. Hostels, cafés, restaurants, warehouses and farms in tourist regions hire working holiday makers all year round — and the sooner you start earning, the further your savings will stretch.
