Can you afford a child in Germany? All costs and benefits calculated for 2026

Calculating the costs and benefits of having a child in Germany as an expat, including financial support and tax benefits. Understanding the expenses and advantages to make an informed decision.

📅 May 11, 2026 ⏱ 3 min read ✍ ExpatScore Editorial
← Back to Blog

Deciding whether to have a child as an expat in Germany is a big decision — and the financial side matters. The short answer for most expat families: yes, probably — if you plan around three things: the parental-leave income drop, your city's childcare fees, and possibly a larger apartment. Here are the real 2026 numbers.

Introduction to Child-Related Costs in Germany

As a planning estimate, a baby in Germany runs to roughly €250–€400/month in everyday costs (food, nappies, clothing, gear) before childcare and housing; a school-age child to around €350–€550/month. Add one-off first baby equipment (Erstausstattung) — pram, cot, car seat, clothes — typically €1,000–€2,500 buying the basics new, and much less second-hand.

Against that stands one of Europe's most generous family support systems: €259/month in Kindergeld, Elterngeld up to €1,800/month during parental leave, free health co-insurance, free public schooling, and childcare that is free or heavily subsidised in many cities. What decides affordability is less the everyday items than your city, housing, and parental-leave setup.

Childcare Costs in Germany

Childcare is the single biggest variable in a young family's budget — and the one where your postcode matters most. Every child has a legal right to a childcare place from age one, but what parents pay is set by the state and city — and the differences are dramatic:

  • Berlin: Kita is free. Parents pay only the meal contribution (~€23/month).
  • Hamburg: Basic childcare is free — for under-3s up to 8 hours/day including lunch; longer hours are income-based.
  • Munich (Bavaria): Municipal fees are income-based and commonly run to a few hundred euros per month. Bavaria softens this with a €100/month childcare subsidy (Beitragszuschuss) for 1- to 2-year-olds in daycare, plus Familiengeld of €250/month for children in their 2nd and 3rd year of life — Bavaria-only, paid regardless of income.

Two warnings. First, "free" does not mean "easy to get": waiting lists in big cities are long, so register during pregnancy. Second, whatever you pay is partly tax-deductible (80%, up to €4,800 per child per year); see Financial Support below.

Types of Childcare in Germany

  • Kita (daycare centre): €0 in Berlin (plus ~€23/month meals) to a few hundred euros/month in fee-charging cities
  • Tagesmutter (registered childminder): usually similar to a local Kita place
  • Nanny (employed privately): €1,500–€2,500 per month — the premium option
  • Parent-child groups: often free or low-cost

Education Costs in Germany

Education is where Germany is genuinely cheap. Public schools are free — tuition and schooling cost nothing, for expat children too. Parents budget instead for the things around school: supplies, lunches, class trips, and optional extras such as music lessons, sports clubs or tutoring (Nachhilfe).

The one big exception is a deliberate choice: private and international schools, typically €5,000–€20,000 or more per year. Most long-term expats use the free public system; the international route suits families expecting to move again.

Education-Related Expenses

  • School materials: €100–€300 per year
  • School lunches: €50–€100 per month
  • Clubs, activities and trips: €50–€200 per month, optional
  • Private / international tuition (optional): €5,000–€20,000+ per year

Healthcare Costs in Germany

Healthcare is the cost that worries expats most — and the one that usually turns out to be zero. If at least one parent has public (statutory) insurance, children are co-insured completely free under Familienversicherung: no extra premium, including all scheduled paediatric check-ups (U-Untersuchungen) and vaccinations. Co-insurance runs until 18, and up to 25 in school, training or studies. Our guide to TK health insurance for expats shows how family co-insurance works with one of the largest public insurers.

The exception is private insurance (PKV), where each child needs a separate policy with its own premium, varying by tariff and provider. What you never need — despite older guides — is a separate "family policy" costing several hundred euros a month: your GKV contribution already covers your children.

Health Insurance Options

  • Statutory insurance (GKV) with Familienversicherung: €0 extra — children co-insured free under a parent
  • Private insurance (PKV): each child needs their own policy; premium varies by tariff and provider

Financial Support and Tax Benefits

This is the section that changes the maths — four programmes do most of the work.

Kindergeld (child benefit) — €259 per month. The Familienkasse pays €259 per child per month in 2026 — €3,108 per year — from the month of birth until at least age 18, and up to 25 if your child is in education or training. The amount is the same for every child and does not depend on your income. It is not automatic: you apply after the birth, and back payments are limited to 6 months, so apply early. Our dedicated guide covers eligibility and the application step by step: Kindergeld in Germany 2026: the full guide.

Elterngeld (parental allowance) — up to €1,800 per month. Elterngeld replaces about 65% of your pre-birth net income while you are on parental leave — low earners get a higher rate, up to 100% — capped at a minimum of €300 and a maximum of €1,800 per month. Basiselterngeld runs 12 months, plus 2 partner months if the other parent also takes leave (14 in total); ElterngeldPlus pays half the rate for roughly twice as long and suits part-time work. Worked example: on a net income of €2,200 per month before the birth, Basiselterngeld pays roughly €1,430 per month (65% of €2,200). Two limits: no Elterngeld above a taxable income of €175,000 (couples and single parents alike), and both parents can take Basiselterngeld simultaneously for only one month in the first 12. Employed mothers usually also receive Mutterschaftsgeld (maternity pay) around the birth, credited against Elterngeld initially.

Kinderzuschlag (child supplement) — up to €297 per month. Working families on lower incomes can receive up to €297 per child per month on top of Kindergeld, also from the Familienkasse — for parents whose income covers their own needs but not the full cost of their children. It is worth checking even if you think you earn "too much", because your rent counts in the calculation.

Tax relief: Kinderfreibetrag and the childcare deduction. Higher earners benefit through the tax system instead. The Kinderfreibetrag is €9,756 per child in 2026 for jointly assessed parents, and you never apply for it separately: your tax return triggers an automatic comparison against your Kindergeld (the Günstigerprüfung), crediting whichever is worth more. Independently, you can deduct 80% of childcare costs, up to €4,800 per child per year, as special expenses (Sonderausgaben) — so keep every Kita invoice.

Financial Support and Tax Benefits

  • Kindergeld: €259/month per child (€3,108/year)
  • Elterngeld: ~65% of prior net, €300–€1,800/month; 12 + 2 partner months
  • Kinderzuschlag: up to €297/month per child, on top of Kindergeld
  • Kinderfreibetrag: €9,756 per child, checked automatically at tax time
  • Childcare deduction: 80% of costs, up to €4,800/child/year

The lesson is not that a child is nearly free — it is that the spread between cities is enormous, and state support absorbs the items (childcare, healthcare, schooling) that dominate family budgets elsewhere. The genuinely large costs are usually indirect: a bigger flat, and reduced earnings during longer leave or part-time years.

Conclusion and Next Steps

So, can you afford a child in Germany? For most expat families with a stable job, yes — provided you plan the parental-leave income dip and your city's childcare rules early. The state's contribution is substantial: €259/month in Kindergeld until adulthood, up to €1,800/month in Elterngeld, free family health cover, free public schools, childcare from free to income-based. Work through the applications in this order:

  1. During pregnancy: start the Kita search — waiting lists are long, and in many places you can register before the birth.
  2. Right after the birth: register the birth and, with birth certificate and your child's tax ID, apply for Kindergeld at the Familienkasse (back payments capped at 6 months).
  3. Within the first months: apply for Elterngeld locally — backdated for a maximum of 3 months, so do not leave the form on the kitchen table.
  4. With your tax return: claim the childcare deduction (80%, up to €4,800) and let the Günstigerprüfung check the €9,756 Kinderfreibetrag.
  5. If money is tight: check Kinderzuschlag (up to €297 per month) on top of Kindergeld.
