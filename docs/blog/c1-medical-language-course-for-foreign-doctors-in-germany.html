How to Get a C1 Medical Language Course in Germany as a Foreign Doctor

As a foreign doctor in Germany, one exam stands between you and your Approbation: the Fachsprachenprüfung, the C1 test of medical German run by your state medical chamber. This guide explains the exam format, the fees, who is exempt, the telc alternative — and how to prepare, possibly for free.

If you studied medicine outside the German-speaking world, one exam stands between you and working as a doctor in Germany: the Fachsprachenprüfung — the C1-level test of medical German run by the state medical chambers (Ärztekammern). It is not a course, and it is not optional: without a pass, the state authority will not grant your Approbation, the full licence to practise medicine. This guide explains who must take the exam, what its three parts involve, what it costs, who is exempt, the telc alternative — and how to prepare, possibly for free.

Why foreign doctors need C1 medical German

The Approbation is Germany's full, unrestricted licence to practise medicine. When you apply for it with a medical degree from a non-German-speaking country, the competent state authority (in Berlin, for example, the LAGeSo) examines two separate things: whether your medical training is equivalent, and whether your German is good enough for safe practice. On the language side, everyday German is not enough — you must demonstrate professional medical language at C1 level.

In practice this is a two-step ladder. Admission to the Fachsprachenprüfung requires a general German certificate at B2 level — some chambers ask you to present the B2 certificate before they will even register you. The Fachsprachenprüfung itself then tests medical communication at C1: understanding patients who describe symptoms in everyday words, explaining a diagnosis and the next steps clearly, documenting a case in writing, and discussing it with a colleague in correct technical language. Legally, what counts is passing this exam. A C1 medical course is the preparation for it — not a substitute.

What the Fachsprachenprüfung actually is

The exam is organised by the Ärztekammer (medical chamber) of the federal state in which you have applied for your Approbation. It is an individual exam of about 60 minutes, conducted by at least two examiners, at least one of whom is a doctor. It has three parts:

  1. Doctor–patient conversation. You meet a simulated patient, take a medical history (Anamnese), and then explain the findings, the suspected diagnosis and the next steps — in plain language a layperson understands, without hiding behind technical terms.
  2. Written documentation. You write up the same case as a short medical report (Arztbrief): structured, complete, and with correct specialist terminology.
  3. Doctor–doctor conversation. You present the case to a colleague — one of the examiners — hand over the key facts and answer questions, this time using precise medical terminology.

The examiners are assessing one ability in three forms: can this doctor communicate safely — empathetically and understandably with patients, precisely with colleagues, and correctly in writing? The outcome is a pass or a fail, and without a pass your Approbation file stays incomplete.

Who is exempt from the Fachsprachenprüfung?

Not every foreign-trained doctor has to sit the exam. You are usually exempt if you completed at least ten years of schooling at a German-language school, or at least three years of vocational training in German. Whether an exemption applies in your case is decided by the state authority handling your Approbation — not by the chamber and not by a language school. Ask for the decision in writing and keep it with your application documents.

Step by step: from Approbation application to exam day

The Fachsprachenprüfung is not a standalone test you can simply book online — it is embedded in the Approbation procedure. The sequence is:

  1. Apply for the Approbation with the competent state authority (for example the LAGeSo in Berlin) and submit your recognition documents.
  2. Wait for the authority's confirmation of receipt. You generally need this confirmation before the Ärztekammer will accept your registration for the exam.
  3. Have your B2 certificate ready. Proof of general German at B2 level is the admission requirement, and some chambers ask to see it at registration.
  4. Register for the Fachsprachenprüfung at the Ärztekammer of that state and pay the exam fee.
  5. Prepare while you wait — ideally in a C1 medical language course (Fachsprachkurs). In busy Länder the wait for an appointment can be several months, so use that time.
  6. Sit the exam (about 60 minutes, three parts). The chamber records the result for your Approbation procedure. If you fail, you can register again — see the FAQ below.

Only when the language requirement is satisfied — and your training recognised as equivalent, or any additional exam passed — will the authority issue the Approbation.

Costs and funding

Exam fee. The Fachsprachenprüfung fee is set by each Bundesland and is roughly €420–€600 (recent Berlin examples ranged from €420 to €550). If you fail and retake the exam, the fee falls due again for every attempt.

Course costs. Private C1 medical courses (Fachsprachkurse) are offered by language schools in most larger cities and online. Prices and durations vary widely by school and course length, so compare several offers — and check the course prepares specifically for the Fachsprachenprüfung, not general C1.

Free preparation via BAMF. Before paying privately, check the funded route: the Federal Office for Migration and Refugees (BAMF) runs Berufssprachkurse (job-related language courses) for academic health professions that take doctors up to C1 level. If you are registered with the Jobcenter or the Agentur für Arbeit, you can obtain a Berechtigungsschein (entitlement voucher) that makes such a course free of charge. Ask there first — many doctors pay for a private course simply because nobody told them the funded option exists.

Quick tip: Ask the Jobcenter or Agentur für Arbeit about a Berechtigungsschein for a BAMF Berufssprachkurs for academic health professions before you sign a private course contract — with the voucher, your C1 medical preparation can be completely free.

telc Deutsch B2·C1 Medizin: the alternative — with a warning

There is a second certificate on the market: telc Deutsch B2·C1 Medizin, a standardised exam with a fee of around €220–€250. It is sometimes presented as a full substitute for the chamber exam. Treat that claim with caution.

In reality, telc replaces the Fachsprachenprüfung in only a few Länder — Saarland and Schleswig-Holstein are examples that accept it. In most Länder, the medical chamber's own Fachsprachenprüfung remains mandatory, and some Länder restrict telc acceptance to doctors who trained in the EU, EEA or Switzerland. The rules are state-specific and they change, so before booking a telc exam, get written confirmation from the state authority handling your Approbation that the certificate will be accepted in your case. A cheaper exam that your authority does not accept is an expensive mistake.

Fachsprachenprüfung vs. Kenntnisprüfung vs. Berufserlaubnis

These three terms are often confused, but they are three different things. The Fachsprachenprüfung tests your language; the Kenntnisprüfung (knowledge exam) tests your medical knowledge. The Kenntnisprüfung is only required if the state authority examines your degree and finds your training not equivalent to a German medical degree. It has nothing to do with your German level: passing the Fachsprachenprüfung does not exempt you from it, and passing it would not replace the language exam.

The Berufserlaubnis is something else again: a temporary, restricted licence — valid for up to two years — that allows a doctor to work in a limited scope, typically while the recognition process is still running. It is not a shortcut around the language requirement: in most Länder, the Fachsprachenprüfung is also required before a Berufserlaubnis is granted. The details vary by Land, so confirm the current rules with your authority.

How to prepare for the Fachsprachenprüfung

Prepare for what is actually assessed. The examiners want to see three registers of German, all at C1 level:

  • Patient German: taking a history, asking follow-up questions, and explaining findings, risks and treatment in words a layperson understands.
  • Professional German: presenting a case to a colleague with precise terminology and a clear structure.
  • Written German: documenting a case as a short, structured medical report with correct specialist vocabulary.

The most effective preparation mirrors the exam itself: practise Anamnese dialogues with a partner or tutor, rehearse explaining common diagnoses in plain language, and write practice Arztbriefe under time pressure. A dedicated C1 Fachsprachkurs is built exactly around these three tasks — and, as described above, it may be fully funded through a BAMF Berufssprachkurs. Finally, plan your timeline realistically: exam appointments in busy Länder can involve waiting times of several months, so register as soon as your application allows and prepare in parallel, not afterwards.
