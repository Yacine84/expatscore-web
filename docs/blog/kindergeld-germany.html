Kindergeld in Germany 2026
€259 per child, every month — who qualifies, how to apply, and the deadline that costs late applicants real money

Germany pays every eligible family €259 per month for each child — and most expat parents qualify. This guide explains exactly who is eligible (and which residence permits are not), how to apply at the Familienkasse, and how the payments work.

What is Kindergeld?

Kindergeld is Germany's monthly child benefit. It is paid by the Familienkasse (Family Benefits Office) of the Federal Employment Agency (Bundesagentur für Arbeit) to parents or guardians for every child living in their household — currently €259 per child per month.

Three things make Kindergeld different from most benefits: it is not means-tested (your income does not matter), it is not taxable income, and it is paid to only one person per child — normally the parent the child lives with. Its legal purpose is to keep a child's minimum living costs tax-free: the state either pays it out monthly as Kindergeld, or grants the equivalent as a tax allowance (the Kinderfreibetrag). You never have to choose — the tax office compares both automatically, as explained below.

Kindergeld is also one of the first benefits new arrivals should sort out: for a family with two children it is worth €6,216 per year, and the application is free.

How much is Kindergeld in 2026?

Since January 2026 the rate is a flat €259 per child per month — up from €255 in 2025. There has been no tiering by birth order since 2023: the first child gets exactly the same as the fourth. If your child is born on the last day of a month, you still receive the full amount for that month, and rate increases are applied automatically — no new application is needed.

Who is eligible for Kindergeld?

You generally qualify if you live in Germany, your child lives in your household, and you hold the right status. For expats, your citizenship and residence title decide:

  • German, EU/EEA and Swiss citizens living in Germany: eligible on the same terms — no extra conditions.
  • Non-EU citizens with a residence permit that authorises employment — for example an EU Blue Card, a skilled-worker residence permit, or a settlement permit (Niederlassungserlaubnis): eligible.
  • Recognised refugees and persons entitled to asylum: eligible.
  • Nationals of Algeria, Bosnia-Herzegovina, Kosovo, Morocco, Montenegro, Serbia, Tunisia or Turkey who are legally employed in Germany and paying social-security contributions: can also qualify under bilateral social-security agreements.
  • Not eligible: parents whose only title is a student residence permit (§16b AufenthG), a job-seeker visa without work authorisation, or an asylum application that is still in progress.

Children living abroad: if your child lives in another EU/EEA country — for example with the other parent — the child can still count for your Kindergeld under EU coordination rules. Which country pays first depends on where each parent lives and works, and Germany may pay a top-up if the other country's benefit is lower. Declare the full situation in your application and let the Familienkasse decide.

Until what age is Kindergeld paid?

  • Up to 18: always, with no further conditions.
  • 18 to 21: if your child is unemployed and registered as job-seeking with the Agentur für Arbeit.
  • 18 to 25: if your child is in school, vocational training (Ausbildung), university studies, or a recognised voluntary service such as an FSJ or Bundesfreiwilligendienst. Transition gaps of up to 4 months between two stages (for example between school and university) remain covered.
  • No age limit: for children whose disability began before their 25th birthday and who cannot support themselves.

Your child's own earnings do not reduce Kindergeld during their first education. For children over 18, the Familienkasse will periodically ask for proof — an enrolment certificate (Immatrikulationsbescheinigung) or the training contract. Send it promptly: payments pause if the proof is missing.

How to apply for Kindergeld — step by step

  1. Register your address and get your tax IDs. You need your own Steuer-ID and your child's Steuer-ID. A newborn's tax ID is posted to you automatically after the birth is registered; after an Anmeldung, a child who moved to Germany also receives one by post. See our Steuer-ID guide if yours never arrived.
  2. Complete the application. The fastest route is online via the Familienkasse section of arbeitsagentur.de — after a birth in Germany, many parents even receive an invitation letter with a pre-filled online form. On paper, the forms are called Antrag auf Kindergeld plus one Anlage Kind per child.
  3. Attach the documents listed in the checklist below.
  4. Send it to your regional Familienkasse. The office responsible depends on your postcode; the online portal routes your application automatically.
  5. Receive your decision (Bescheid) and Kindergeldnummer. Processing usually takes a few weeks. Keep the number safe — you need it for every later contact, and its last digit sets your payment day.

When will the money arrive? Payment dates explained

Kindergeld is paid once a month, but not on the same day for everyone. Your personal payment day is fixed by the last digit of your Kindergeldnummer: numbers ending in 0 are paid at the very beginning of the month, and each higher digit is paid a little later, with 9 at the end of the month. The Familienkasse publishes the exact payment calendar for each year, so once you know your number you can predict every payment date. If you also receive Kinderzuschlag, it arrives on the same day, in the same transfer.

Kindergeld or Kinderfreibetrag — which is better?

You do not have to choose, and you cannot apply for the allowance separately. When you file your German tax return, the tax office automatically runs the Günstigerprüfung ("more favourable check"): it calculates whether the Kindergeld you received, or the Kinderfreibetrag — a tax allowance of €9,756 per child in 2026 for jointly assessed parents (half per parent) — saves you more, and credits you the difference in your tax assessment.

As a rule of thumb, Kindergeld is worth more for most families; the allowance only pulls ahead at higher taxable incomes. Either way, two things are essential: apply for Kindergeld itself (the check builds on it), and file a tax return so the comparison actually happens.

Extra help on a low income: Kinderzuschlag (up to €297)

If you work but your income covers your own needs and not quite your children's, you may qualify for the Kinderzuschlag on top of Kindergeld: up to €297 per child per month in 2026. It is applied for at the same Familienkasse, paid on the same day as Kindergeld, and often combined with Wohngeld (housing benefit). 5 mistakes that cost parents money

  1. Applying late. The 6-month backdating cap is absolute — every extra month of delay is €259 per child lost forever.
  2. Not reporting changes. Your child finishes school or training, your family moves abroad, you separate — the Familienkasse must be told immediately. Unreported changes lead to repayment demands (Erstattungsbescheid), sometimes for thousands of euros.
  3. Assuming a student permit qualifies. It does not. Parents who are in Germany only on a §16b student permit have no Kindergeld claim, even with a part-time job.
  4. Forgetting the proof for adult children. No enrolment certificate, no payment — Familienkasse pauses 18+ payments until the proof arrives.
  5. Name mismatches. Names must match exactly across passports, birth certificates and the application. A different spelling or name order is one of the most common causes of processing delays.
